Twenty-eight thousand years ago, a lion cub died in Siberia.
It was barely a month old.
The ground froze around it, and it stayed frozen. In 2018, it came out of the riverbank still covered in fur. Its paws, teeth and even its whiskers were intact. Scientists named it Sparta.
Sparta was a cave lion. If it had lived, it would have grown into one of the largest cats that ever walked the Earth.
And it shared the Ice Age with something even stranger.
Human beings.
Our ancestors lived alongside cave lions, saber-toothed cats, giant bears and wolves heavier than any alive today. They didn't watch these animals from a safe distance. They hunted where these predators hunted and slept where they prowled.
So here's the question.
If you were dropped into the Ice Age with nothing but your bare hands, which of these predators could you actually beat?
Let's start with the one that seems the most beatable.
And work our way up to the ones that weren't even close.
In the middle of Los Angeles, there's a place where the ground is still sticky.
The La Brea Tar Pits.
For tens of thousands of years, natural asphalt seeped up through the soil there. Animals walked into it and got stuck. Then predators came to eat the trapped animals, and they got stuck too.
When scientists started digging, they expected a mix of animals.
What they found was a wall of predators.
The most common large mammal at La Brea isn't a bison or a horse. It's the dire wolf. Researchers have recovered the remains of thousands of them.
At first, dire wolves were thought to be a kind of oversized gray wolf.
That turned out to be wrong.
In 2021, scientists analyzed ancient DNA from dire wolf bones. The results surprised everyone. Dire wolves weren't close relatives of gray wolves at all. Their lineage split off millions of years ago and evolved on its own in the Americas.
Physically, a dire wolf was similar in size to a large gray wolf, but heavier and more solidly built. It probably weighed around sixty kilograms. Its skull was broad, and its teeth were built for crushing bone.
So could you beat one?
Honestly, even this is a bad matchup.
A sixty-kilogram animal with a bone-crushing bite would be faster than you, and it would have a weapon you don't. Your best hope would be to protect your throat with your arm and fight back with everything you have.
Some people have survived wolf attacks this way. It's possible.
But there's a much bigger problem.
Dire wolves almost certainly lived in packs. You wouldn't be fighting one wolf.
You'd be fighting all of them.
And they're not even the most dangerous animal from that tar pit.
Across Ice Age Europe and Asia, another predator was cracking bones.
In 1821, workers at a quarry in Yorkshire, England, broke into a cave called Kirkdale. Inside, the floor was covered in bones. There were elephants, rhinos and hippos. Bones from animals no one expected to find in England.
Many of the bones were gnawed. Some were split open.
The geologist William Buckland investigated. He noticed the bite marks and the teeth mixed in with the remains, and he realized what had happened.
This cave was a hyena den.
For generations, hyenas had dragged their prey inside and eaten it there.
These were cave hyenas. They were closely related to today's spotted hyenas, but larger and heavier. And spotted hyenas today are already among the most powerful biters in the animal world, capable of cracking bones that a lion leaves behind.
So could you beat a cave hyena?
Probably not. A heavier hyena with jaws that could shatter a rhino bone wouldn't need many bites to end the fight.
And like the dire wolf, hyenas were social. Spotted hyenas today live in clans that can number in the dozens.
But these hunters relied on numbers.
The next predator didn't need them.
Back at La Brea, the second most common predator is the one everyone recognizes.
Smilodon. The saber-toothed cat.
Its upper canines were longer than a human hand. Its body was heavily muscled, especially in the front legs and neck. The California species, Smilodon fatalis, likely weighed somewhere around two hundred kilograms or more. In South America, its relative, Smilodon populator, may have reached around four hundred.
Here's the strange part.
Despite those enormous teeth, Smilodon had a fairly weak bite for a cat its size.
So how did it kill?
Scientists think it used its powerful forelimbs to pin prey down first. Only then, once the animal couldn't struggle, did it drive its long canines into the throat. The teeth were deadly, but also fragile. They could break if the prey thrashed too hard.
That sounds like a weakness. And in a way, it is.
But for you, it doesn't help much. Smilodon's arms alone were strong enough to hold down horses and bison. A human would be pinned in seconds.
And there's another detail that makes Smilodon even more unsettling.
Some skeletons from La Brea show severe injuries that had healed. Damaged hips. Broken bones that would have made hunting nearly impossible for a long time.
Yet these cats survived.
Some researchers think that means other Smilodon helped them, perhaps by sharing food. Others argue a wounded cat could have survived by scavenging alone. The debate isn't settled.
But if Smilodon lived in groups, you might never meet just one.
And Smilodon wasn't the only saber-tooth.
In 2020, near a river in Yakutia, in far eastern Siberia, a frozen animal emerged from the permafrost.
It was small. Covered in dark fur. Its face was preserved so well it almost looked asleep.
At first glance, it looked like it could be a lion cub.
But when scientists studied it, they realized it was something no one had ever seen in the flesh before.
It was a Homotherium cub.
Homotherium was a scimitar-toothed cat. Its teeth were shorter and broader than Smilodon's, and its body was built differently. It had long front legs and a sloping back, more like a hyena's. The researchers estimated that the cub was about three weeks old when it died, more than thirty-five thousand years ago.
For more than a century, scientists only knew Homotherium from bones.
Now they could see its fur, its paws and even the shape of its mouth.
The cub's neck was thick and powerful. Its front legs were already sturdy. Even at three weeks old, its body showed the kind of predator it was going to become.
Researchers think adult Homotherium may have hunted in groups, possibly going after large prey. Some scientists have even suggested it took on young mammoths, though that idea is still debated.
Could you beat one?
No.
Not as a pack, and almost certainly not alone either. A cat built to bring down prey many times your size wouldn't need to try very hard.
But there were still bigger cats out there.
Which brings us back to Sparta.
The cave lion lived across Europe and northern Asia. It was larger than modern African lions. Males may have weighed well over two hundred kilograms, and some estimates go higher.
We know what these lions looked like better than almost any other Ice Age predator, because early humans painted them.
In Chauvet Cave in France, more than thirty thousand years ago, people drew lions on the walls. They drew them hunting. They drew them in groups.
And something about those drawings stands out.
The male lions don't seem to have the large manes of modern African lions.
That suggests something important. These people weren't drawing from imagination. They were drawing animals they had seen.
Maybe too closely.
Could you beat a cave lion?
No. A modern African lion can bring down a buffalo. A bigger lion adapted to the cold, hunting reindeer, horses and bison, would make a fight with an unarmed human very short.
And yet, there's still one more animal.
One that, when it stood up, might have looked down on all of them.
The short-faced bear lived in North America.
For decades, it had a terrifying reputation. With its long legs and short, wide face, scientists once imagined it as a super-predator. A running bear that chased down prey across open plains.
Large individuals may have weighed close to a ton. Standing on its hind legs, it may have reached more than three meters tall.
But then researchers looked more closely.
Chemical studies of its bones, along with analyses of its skull and teeth, started to challenge the old picture. Some scientists now think the short-faced bear may have eaten a mix of meat and plants, like many bears today. Others suggest it may have scavenged, using its sheer size to drive other predators off their kills.
The debate continues.
But for you, the details don't matter.
An animal that may have weighed close to a ton, with claws and teeth to match, doesn't need to be a specialist hunter to win.
Even the predators that killed mammoths might have backed away from it.
So, which Ice Age predator could you actually beat?
The honest answer is none of them.
Not the wolves. Not the hyenas. Not the cats. And definitely not the bear.
But that's not the end of the story.
Because humans didn't just survive in this world.
We stayed.
Early humans didn't fight these animals with their bare hands. They used spears. They used fire. They planned, they cooperated and they passed knowledge down from one generation to the next.
And something strange happened over the next thousands of years.
The dire wolf disappeared. Smilodon disappeared. Homotherium, the cave lion, the cave hyena and the short-faced bear all disappeared.
Why?
Scientists still argue about it. Climate change at the end of the Ice Age clearly played a role. But many researchers think humans did too, by hunting the same prey these predators depended on, and sometimes by hunting the predators themselves.
In the end, the weakest animal in this whole story was the only one still standing.
Sparta, the frozen cub, was buried long before any of this happened.
It never got to see what its world became.
A world where the most dangerous predator of the Ice Age wasn't the one with the biggest teeth.
It was the one painting the others on the walls.
