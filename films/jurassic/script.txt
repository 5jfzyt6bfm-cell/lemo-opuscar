The weapons wouldn't be the first problem.
The first problem would be the silence.
No birdsong. No grass. No flowers. Just wind moving through ferns, the smell of resin and rotting wood, and somewhere in the distance a sound no living person has ever heard. A low, rumbling call from an animal heavier than a loaded truck.
Now imagine one hundred modern soldiers standing in the middle of that.
They have rifles, body armor, radios, and training. They have one mission. Survive for seven days.
They are about 150 million years in the past.
And everything around them has spent millions of years evolving without ever seeing a human.
The place is western North America during the Late Jurassic. Today, the rocks from this time are called the Morrison Formation. They stretch across Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, and Montana. They are one of the richest dinosaur graveyards ever found.
That matters, because it means we know a surprising amount about where our soldiers would land.
We know which predators lived there. We know which plants grew there. We even know some of the injuries those animals suffered.
So the question isn't just whether a hundred soldiers could survive.
It's which part of this world would kill them first.
The first hours would feel strangely calm.
The landscape would look half familiar and half wrong. There would be wide floodplains, slow rivers, and forests of conifers. There would be tree ferns, cycads with thick trunks, ginkgo trees, and huge stands of horsetails along the water.
But something important would be missing.
There would be no grass anywhere. Grasses hadn't evolved yet. There would be no flowering plants either. Every bit of green the soldiers could see would come from older groups of plants.
And the air itself would be different.
Scientists believe carbon dioxide levels in the Jurassic were several times higher than today. Oxygen levels are harder to pin down. Some models suggest slightly less than now, others suggest more. Either way, the air would almost certainly be breathable.
But high carbon dioxide could still make some soldiers feel heavy and tired. And the weather would be hot. The Morrison region was likely semi-arid, with long dry seasons broken by wet ones.
That makes the first real enemy obvious.
Water.
A hundred soldiers need a lot of it. In heat like this, each person could need several liters a day just to function. That's hundreds of liters for the group, every single day.
The rivers would provide it. Water purification tablets and filters could make it safer.
But every animal in the region would need that same water.
And the rivers were exactly where the predators waited.
Food would be the second problem.
The soldiers might carry a few days of rations. After that, they would need to find something to eat.
And this is where the Jurassic becomes quietly dangerous.
Many of the plants around them would be poisonous or close to useless. Cycads contain toxins that can damage the liver and nervous system. Some ferns and horsetails contain a compound that destroys vitamin B1 in the body. Eat enough of them, and your nerves and muscles start to fail.
So the safest food wouldn't be plants.
It would be animals.
The rivers held fish, including lungfish. There were also small mammals, lizards, and early relatives of crocodiles. Hunting small animals would be possible.
But to feed a hundred people, the soldiers might start thinking about something bigger.
And in the Morrison Formation, there was plenty of something bigger.
On the second day, they would almost certainly see them.

Sauropods.
Diplodocus, Apatosaurus, Brachiosaurus. Animals with necks longer than a school bus and bodies that could weigh as much as several elephants combined. Some could stretch more than twenty-five meters from head to tail.
To the soldiers, these giants might look peaceful.
They would be wrong.
A sauropod didn't need to attack a human to kill one. It only needed to step in the wrong place.
And some scientists think their tails could be used as defensive weapons. In the 1990s, researchers suggested a Diplodocus tail tip might even break the sound barrier. Later studies questioned that idea. A 2022 computer model suggested the tail moved fast, but probably not supersonic.
Still, a whip of muscle and bone that long, moving that fast, would not be something anyone wants to stand behind.
But the sauropods weren't the real threat.
The real threat was the animal that hunted them.
Imagine the soldiers set up camp near the river on the third night. They dig in. They post sentries. They use night vision.
And then something walks out of the trees.
Allosaurus.
Around eight to nine meters long. Long legs, a big skull, and curved, serrated teeth built to slice flesh. It was the most common large predator of the Morrison Formation.
And we know just how common it was because of one strange place in Utah.
The Cleveland-Lloyd Dinosaur Quarry.
When paleontologists began digging there, they found bones. Thousands of them. That alone wasn't unusual.
What was unusual was who those bones belonged to.
Most of them came from predators.
Researchers have identified the remains of dozens of Allosaurus at the site. At least forty-six individuals, of different ages, all packed together in one place.
In most fossil sites, plant-eaters vastly outnumber meat-eaters. Here, the pattern was flipped.
What happened?
Scientists still don't fully agree. Some think the animals were trapped in mud. Some think they gathered around shrinking water during a drought and died there over time. Others suspect the bones were collected by floods.
We still don't know for sure.
But for our soldiers, the lesson is simple. Allosaurus wasn't rare. And it may have been drawn to the same water they depended on.
So how would modern weapons do against it?
This is where things get uncomfortable.
A standard infantry rifle fires a small, fast bullet. It's designed to stop people, not multi-ton animals. Hunters usually choose much larger calibers just for elk and bear.
A single rifle shot would probably not stop an Allosaurus.
But one hundred trained soldiers don't fire single shots.
Concentrated fire from many rifles, especially with machine guns in the mix, could bring down even a large predator, particularly with hits to the head or chest. A company that stayed together, kept watch, and reacted fast would have a real chance.
The danger is everything that comes before the shooting.
Allosaurus may have relied on ambush. Some researchers think it attacked with its upper jaw like a hatchet, using quick slashing bites. A soldier on watch at the tree line might get only a few seconds of warning.
And we have proof of how tough these animals were.
In 1991, a team in Wyoming uncovered a remarkably complete Allosaurus skeleton. They nicknamed it Big Al.
At first, it looked like a beautiful specimen.
Then they looked closer at the bones.
Big Al was covered in injuries. Broken ribs. Damaged vertebrae. Infected bones in the foot. Scientists have counted around nineteen separate injuries or infections.
Some of those wounds had healed.
This animal kept living and hunting while carrying damage that would have stopped most creatures.
Which raises a terrifying possibility for the soldiers.
A wounded Allosaurus might not run away.
And Allosaurus wasn't the only predator out there.
The Morrison Formation was also home to Ceratosaurus, a predator with a horn on its nose and blade-like teeth. And there was Torvosaurus, which may have been even bigger than Allosaurus, with a massive skull and powerful arms.
There were smaller hunters too, like Ornitholestes, roughly the size of a large dog.
But if the soldiers want to know what a dangerous plant-eater looks like, they only need to meet Stegosaurus.
At first, it might seem slow and harmless. Big plates along its back. A small head close to the ground.
Then it turns.
At the end of its tail are four long spikes.
For years, scientists debated whether those spikes were really weapons or just for display.
Then researchers examined an Allosaurus tail bone with a strange wound in it. A hole had been punched straight through the bone.
When they compared it to a Stegosaurus spike, the shape matched.
The wound showed signs of infection, suggesting the Allosaurus lived for some time afterward. But the message was clear.
Stegosaurus fought back. And it hit hard enough to pierce bone.
If an animal could do that to a predator built for combat, a soldier in body armor wouldn't stand a chance at close range.
By day five, the real problem wouldn't be any single animal.
It would be exhaustion.
Ammunition is limited. A standard combat load for a soldier is a few hundred rounds. Every encounter would burn through it. Every night on watch would wear people down. Every trip to the river would be a risk.
And then there's the smallest threat of all.

Infection.
Bacteria have existed for billions of years. A cut from a tooth, a claw, or even a sharp branch could become infected. And with no hospital, even a minor wound could turn serious within days.
No one knows what kinds of microbes lived in the Jurassic, or how a human body would react to them.
But here is where the story flips.
The soldiers wouldn't just be at risk from this world.
This world might be at risk from them.
Every human carries trillions of bacteria. Some live on the skin. Others live in the gut. These microbes evolved alongside mammals over tens of millions of years that hadn't happened yet.
What would happen if those microbes spread into a Jurassic ecosystem that had never encountered them?
We simply don't know.
Most would probably die off. Some might do nothing at all.
But it's a strange thought. The most dangerous thing the soldiers brought back in time might not be their rifles.
It might be invisible.
So could one hundred modern soldiers survive one week in the Jurassic?
Most of them probably could.
If they stayed together, stayed away from the river at night, avoided the sauropods, didn't eat the wrong plants, and treated every shadow at the tree line as a threat, a well-trained group would likely make it seven days.
But not without losses.
And not without learning something deeply humbling.
On day seven, as the soldiers wait to leave, they might look out across the floodplain. Sauropods moving like slow hills. Stegosaurus grazing by the water. Somewhere in the trees, an Allosaurus watching.
None of these animals know what a human is.
None of them know that one day, their bones will be pulled from the ground in Utah and Wyoming and studied by the very species standing in front of them.
For one week, the soldiers would be visitors from the future.
Strange, loud, fragile animals.
In a world that would continue for millions of years without ever noticing they were gone.
